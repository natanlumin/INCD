Attestable and testable properties of an open-source model

What this document answers

An organisation that takes an open-source model (SCRUM-281) into use wants to know things about it: who published it, what it was trained on, what it may be used for, whether the file (SCRUM-281) is the one published, and how it behaves. Some of these it can establish without running the model, from what the producer (SCRUM-281) and the platform expose. The rest it can establish only by running the model. A few it cannot establish at all.

  • Attestable properties: read, not run.
    • They come from the platform, the producer, a signature or a record that travels with the file.
    • Each rests on someone's word or on a cryptographic check, and their strength differs widely.
  • Properties discoverable only by testing: run, not read.
    • They are behaviour, hidden triggers, capability boundaries, robustness and latent contamination.
    • No metadata reveals them, because no one who writes metadata knows them, or because the one who does has a reason not to say.
  • The split is the reason testing is necessary.
    • Everything the organisation needs to know about how the model behaves lies on the second side.
    • What lies on the first side is, at its strongest, a statement of who published the file and that it has not changed since.

This document answers four questions, one per row of Table 1.

Part 1. The split and how strength is rated

1.1 Where the line falls

The glossary distinguishes attestable properties, established from what publisher and platform expose, from testable properties, which no metadata reveals (SCRUM-281, section 1.4). The lifecycle assessment framework splits the attestable side by who vouches for it, and the testable side by what is measured (SCRUM-284, section 1.1, Table 1):

  • guaranteed: the platform enforces or verifies it;
  • declared: the producer (SCRUM-281) states it about the model, or the serving provider (SCRUM-281) about the endpoint, and nothing verifies it;
  • readable: read from the artefact itself, or from the organisation's own deployment of it, with no one's word in between;
  • testable: established only by running the model, on its outputs or on its internals.

A cryptographic signature is a fifth case that the organisation can check itself: whoever holds the key vouches for the file (SCRUM-281), and the check of the signature against the key is the organisation's own. It is treated here as attestable and rated on its own terms.

1.2 What the line depends on

Two things move a property across the line or remove it.

  • Access. By proxy (SCRUM-281), only declared statements and black-box behaviour exist; the platform's guarantees, the file and its signatures do not travel with the key (SCRUM-284, Table 1). A property that is readable in possession (SCRUM-281) becomes, by proxy (SCRUM-281), the serving provider's statement.
  • Derivation. A derivative model (SCRUM-281) declares its parent on its card, and the platform does not verify the relation (SCRUM-286, section 1.1). Every attestable property of the parent, its licence, its training-data statement, its signature, says nothing about the derivative unless the derivative carries its own.

1.3 The evidence-strength rating

Each attestable property is rated on four levels. The rating is of the evidence the property can offer at best, with the reason stated; where the property is often absent, the entry says so.

Rating Meaning
strong the organisation can verify it itself: a cryptographic check against a known key, or a fact the platform guarantees
moderate the record's presence and form can be checked, a structured field or a standard format, but its content is the publisher's statement
weak free text or optional fields, unverified, and in the literature often absent, incomplete or wrong
none nothing exists to check

Each entry also tags the lifecycle stage (SCRUM-281) at which the property is read, and the framework question and evidence item it answers, Qn.k and en.k (SCRUM-284), given in full in the annex, Table 4.

Part 2. Attestable properties

Six properties can be established, to a degree, without running the model. They are treated in the order the assessment of a model meets them.

P1. Publisher identity

Read at: S1, Q1.1, on e1.1 and e1.2 (SCRUM-284).

The repository guarantees which account uploaded a file (SCRUM-281) and when (SCRUM-284, section 1.1). It does not guarantee that the account is the producer (SCRUM-281) whose name appears in the repository's title: organisations group accounts with no identity-proof step on the documentation page (Hugging Face, Organizations) [SC‑29]. The one verification the platform documents is a badge for organisations on its paid plans that verify a domain name and require admin approval for members with matching e-mail addresses (Hugging Face, Advanced Security) [AT-05]. Signed commits bind a commit to a key on the account, treated under P5. A publisher can also plant an identifying response that only it can trigger, a fingerprint, but a third party can test for it only if the publisher planted one (Xu et al., NAACL 2024) [AT-25].

Rating: strong for the uploading account; moderate for an organisation with a verified domain; weak for the producer behind any other account.

P2. Training-data disclosure

Read at: S1, Q1.1 and Q1.2, on e1.2 (SCRUM-284).

What a model was trained on is documented, where it is documented at all, by its producer: the practice of a datasheet records a dataset's motivation, composition and collection process (Gebru et al., Communications of the ACM 2021) [AT-17], and on the primary repository it is declared on the card, in a datasets field the uploader fills in or leaves empty (Hugging Face, Model Cards) [AT-04]; among widely used models only a minority fill it (SCRUM-286, section 2.3). Where card sections are filled, the training section is the most consistently filled and limitations and evaluation the least (Liang et al., Nature Machine Intelligence 2024) [AT-18], but a filled section is a statement, not the data. Among major developers, data access, data labour and copyright status remain systemically opaque (Bommasani et al., TMLR 2025) [AT-19]. Regulation now requires the providers of general-purpose models, open-source ones included, to publish a sufficiently detailed summary of the content used for training, on a template that sets a common minimal baseline (EU AI Act, Article 53(1)(d) and 53(2); European Commission template) [AT-01, AT-02]. The obligation raises how often a statement exists; it does not make the statement verifiable, and a summary is not the data.

Rating: weak. The statement cannot be checked against the file, and whether a given record was in the training data cannot be tested reliably either (Part 5).

P3. Model card

Read at: S1, Q1.5 and Q1.6, on e1.2 (SCRUM-284).

A model card reports a model's intended use and its evaluation across conditions (Mitchell et al., FAT* 2019) [AT-16]. On the primary model repository (SCRUM-281) it is a README with machine-read metadata, licence, datasets, base model, which the platform uses for filters and to infer the relation to a parent (Hugging Face, Model Cards) [AT-04]. The card is best practice, not a requirement (Hugging Face, Getting Started with Repositories) [HF‑15]. The structured fields can be checked for presence and form; their content, the base model included, is the uploader's statement (SCRUM-286, section 1.1). Whether a declared parent is the real one can be tested in part: black-box provenance testing identified derived models with high precision where the derivation preserves output similarity (Nikolic et al. 2025, preprint) [AT-26].

Rating: moderate for the structured fields; weak for the free text.

P4. AI bill of materials

Read at: S1, Q1.2, on e1.4 where it ships with the file, and S3, Q3.2, on e3.3 for what is deployed (SCRUM-284).

What it is. A software bill of materials, SBOM, is "a formal record containing the details and supply chain relationships of various components used in building software"; its minimum data fields name each component, its supplier, its version, its other identifiers and its relationships, and it is meant to be generated and read by machine (NTIA, The Minimum Elements for a Software Bill of Materials, 2021) [AT-27]. An AI bill of materials, AIBOM, applies the same record to a model. The model becomes a component of its own, and the record adds what software has no equivalent for: the datasets the model was trained and evaluated on, its training parameters, its intended use and limitations, and its provenance (CycloneDX ML-BOM; SPDX 3.0.1 AI and Dataset profiles) [AT-06, AT-07].

The two formats. A bill of materials is written in a standard format so that tools can produce and check it; the minimum-elements report names SPDX and CycloneDX among them [AT-27].

  • CycloneDX, published as an international standard by Ecma International (ECMA-424) [AT-28]. Machine-learning models have been a component type since its version 1.5, with a model card section recording datasets, training parameters and provenance (CycloneDX ML-BOM) [SC‑25, AT-06].
  • SPDX, published as an international standard by ISO and IEC (ISO/IEC 5962:2021, as its maintainers state) [AT-29]. Its version 3.0.1 has an AI profile and a dataset profile recording training information, limitations, known bias and data collection (SPDX 3.0.1) [AT-07].

Who asks for one, and who ships one. The secure development profile for generative and foundation models asks producers to track the provenance of a model, its components and derivatives, and to consider disclosing the provenance of training data (NIST SP 800-218A, PS.3.2) [AT-09]. A vendor channel ships one with each image it publishes (NVIDIA NGC) [CG-11]. How many open-source models (SCRUM-281) ship one is not established.

Rating: moderate. The format can be validated; the content is declared by whoever wrote it.

P5. Cryptographic signing and attestation

Read at: S1, Q1.2, on e1.3; again at S3, Q3.1, on e3.1 (SCRUM-284).

A model signature covers a manifest listing every file by its hash, so that a recipient can verify that the model was published by the expected source and has not been changed since (OpenSSF Model Signing) [AT-10, AT-11]. A commit on the primary repository is marked verified when it is signed with a key on the uploading account; unsigned commits carry no status, and git itself does not authenticate commit authors (Hugging Face, Signing commits with GPG) [AT-03, HF‑11]. One vendor signs every model it publishes on its catalogue with the OpenSSF format (NVIDIA NGC and NIM) [AT-12]; a code platform signs build attestations with Sigstore and states that they are not a guarantee that an artefact is secure (GitHub artifact attestations) [AT-13]. The build-provenance standard does not address models or training data (SLSA v1.1) [AT-14]. On the primary model repository, signatures were close to absent in the year measured, about 0.1 per cent of artefacts, and fewer than a quarter of those were valid (Schorlemmer et al., IEEE S&P 2024) [AT-23].

Rating: strong where present, for origin and integrity only. A valid signature establishes who published the files and that they are unchanged; it establishes nothing about what the model does, and it is rarely present.

P6. Licence lineage

Read at: S1, Q1.4, on e1.7 (SCRUM-284).

A licence field can be added to any repository, and unknown and other are valid values (Hugging Face, Licenses) [AT-08]; the field may be left blank (Hugging Face, Getting Started with Repositories) [HF‑15]. Lineage is the chain of licences from a base model through its derivatives, and it runs along the declared parent relation, which nothing verifies (P3). On dataset hosting sites, licences were omitted for more than 70 per cent of datasets and wrong for more than half (Longpre et al., Nature Machine Intelligence 2024) [AT-20]; licences were found inconsistent between models and the projects that use them (Jiang et al., MSR 2024) [AT-21], and in about a third of transitions from a model to an application a restrictive clause was dropped by relicensing (Jewitt et al. 2025, preprint) [AT-22].

Rating: weak.

Part 3. Properties discoverable only by testing

Five properties no metadata reveals. For each: what it is, why no record carries it, how it is tested, and where the test stops. Each is tied to the threats of the threats document it would reveal.

D1. Behavioural properties

Tested at: S1, Q1.6, on e1.9; S2, Q2.3, on e2.5; S3, Q3.3, on e3.5; S4, Q4.3, on e4.4 (SCRUM-284).

What the model does on the inputs the organisation will give it: whether it follows instructions, refuses what its safeguard (SCRUM-281) should refuse, and gives the outputs it gave when assessed. A card states an intended use (Mitchell et al., FAT* 2019) [AT-16]; it cannot state behaviour on inputs its writer never saw. Behaviour is measured black-box, through a file (SCRUM-281) or a key, on the same inputs at every stage, which is what makes stages comparable (SCRUM-284, section 2). It reveals quantisation drift and fine-tune tampering against the parent (T11, T12) and the susceptibility half of direct injection and jailbreaks (T15, T17). The test covers the inputs sent.

D2. Hidden triggers

Tested at: S1, Q1.6, on e1.9 and e1.10; S2, Q2.2, on e2.4 and e2.5 (SCRUM-284).

Behaviour that appears only on a trigger the planter chose (T10), including one that appears only once the model is quantised (T13). No record carries it, since the only party who knows it is the one who planted it. Statistical scanners exist for both. A search can find a trigger in a classifier (Liu et al., IEEE S&P 2022) [TH‑13], and a scan that inverts the backdoor's target rather than its trigger finds backdoors in generative models with black-box access (Shen et al., IEEE S&P 2025) [TH‑53]. Recovering the planted trigger itself in a generative model is close to random (Maloyan et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑14], and detectors fail when the backdoor is trained differently (Yan et al., EMNLP 2025) [TH‑12]. A quantisation trigger is found, if at all, by testing the quantised copy against full precision. A scan gives evidence with an error rate, within the kinds of backdoor and the search it covers: it can find a backdoor, and a clean result lowers the likelihood of one, but it cannot show that none is there.

D3. Capability boundaries

Tested at: S1, Q1.6, on e1.9 and e1.10; S2, Q2.3, on e2.5 (SCRUM-284).

What the model can do when pushed, harmful capabilities included, as opposed to what it does when asked plainly. The producer (SCRUM-281) may state a boundary; the statement is declared. A test elicits the capability and gives a lower bound: models can be made to underperform on capability evaluations on purpose, and models trained to hide a capability behind a password kept it hidden on a hazardous-knowledge benchmark, so that capability evaluations are vulnerable to this underperformance (van der Weij et al., ICLR 2025) [AT-24]. The safeguard's boundary moves under jailbreaks (T17) and is removed by tampering (T11).

D4. Robustness

Tested at: S1, Q1.6, on e1.9 and e1.10; S2, Q2.3 and Q2.4, on e2.5 (SCRUM-284).

Whether the model's behaviour holds under adversarial inputs, changed generation settings and reduced precision. No record carries it, because it depends on inputs and settings the producer did not try. The official taxonomy records that quantised models inherit the original's weaknesses and add their own (NIST AI 100-2, section 4.2.4) [TH‑03], and that changing generation settings alone can break a safeguard (Huang et al., ICLR 2024) [TH‑42]. Robustness is tested against the attacks the tester knows (T16, T17) and holds only for them; every defence in the cited jailbreak literature was broken by a later attack (threats document, T17).

D5. Latent contamination

Tested at: S1, Q1.6, on e1.9 and e1.10; S2, Q2.2 and Q2.3, on e2.4 and e2.5 (SCRUM-284).

What the training put into the model without saying so: poisoned behaviour (T9), a behaviour inherited from a merged parent (T14), and evaluation data seen in training, which inflates the model's published scores. No record carries it, because the training data is declared at best (P2). Poisoning and inherited behaviour are tested as behaviour, D1 and D2. Evaluation contamination is tested by comparing performance across benchmarks, and existing detection methods for it can be evaded and fail to quantify it (Dekoninck et al., NeurIPS 2024) [AT-15]; whether a given record was in the training data cannot be established reliably on large generative models (Duan et al., COLM 2024) [TH‑50].

Two readings of Table 3 follow. Down the strength column: the only strong evidence on the attestable side is the account that uploaded the files, which says who uploaded them and when, and a valid signature, which says who published them and that they are unchanged since. Neither, and nothing else on the attestable side, says what the model does. Down the by-proxy column: an organisation that holds only a key keeps the declared statements and black-box behaviour, and loses every guarantee, every signature and every test of internals.

Part 5. What is neither attestable nor practically testable

Caution. What no one can currently establish about an open-source model (SCRUM-281).

  • The training data actually used. It is declared at best, in a summary if the law requires one (EU AI Act, Article 53; European Commission template) [AT-01, AT-02]; it cannot be read from the file (SCRUM-281); and testing whether a given record was used is not reliable on large generative models (Duan et al., COLM 2024) [TH‑50].
  • That a model carries no trigger, as a certainty. Statistical scanners exist and find backdoors in classifiers and generative models with the error rates their evaluations report (Liu et al., IEEE S&P 2022; Shen et al., IEEE S&P 2025) [TH‑13, TH‑53]. A clean scan is evidence within the kinds of backdoor and the search it covers, not proof: a planted trigger may not be recoverable, and detectors fail on backdoors trained differently (Maloyan et al. 2024, preprint; Yan et al., EMNLP 2025) [TH‑14, TH‑12]. Absence is testable statistically and attestable not at all.
  • The full extent of what a model can do. A capability test gives a lower bound, and a model can be made to hide what it can do (van der Weij et al., ICLR 2025) [AT-24].
  • That benchmark scores were not inflated by contamination. Detection methods can be evaded (Dekoninck et al., NeurIPS 2024) [AT-15].
  • That a signed model is safe. A signature establishes origin and integrity only (Sigstore model-transparency; GitHub artifact attestations) [AT-11, AT-13].
  • The real parent of a derivative that does not declare one, or declares a false one, beyond the statistical evidence of provenance testing where the derivation preserves output similarity (Nikolic et al. 2025, preprint) [AT-26].
  • For a model held only by proxy (SCRUM-281): anything beyond the serving provider's statements and the model's black-box behaviour. The file, its hash, its signature and its internals are out of reach, and which model and version is served is the provider's word (SCRUM-284, Table 1).

The list is the reason the testing methodology is necessary rather than optional. Every declared item of the lifecycle framework is an assessment gap (SCRUM-284, section 2); Part 2 shows that most attestable properties are declared; and Part 3 shows that the properties an organisation most needs, how the model behaves and whether something was planted in it, are on the side no metadata reaches.

Summary: the four questions answered

What can be established without running the model, and how strongly? Two things strongly: which account uploaded the files, which the platform guarantees, and, where the files are signed, that they come from the key holder and are unchanged. The structured fields of a card and a bill of materials moderately, in form but not in content. Training-data disclosure, free-text card claims and licence lineage weakly: they are the producer's word, often absent, and in the cited audits often wrong.

What can be established only by running it? Behaviour, hidden triggers, capability boundaries, robustness and latent contamination. Each is tested against named threats, and each test is bounded: by the inputs sent, by the triggers a search can reach, by the capability a test can elicit, and by the attacks the tester knows.

Which side of the line is each property on? Six attestable, five discoverable (Table 3). By proxy the attestable side shrinks to statements and the discoverable side to outputs.

What can be established neither way today? The training data used, the absence of a trigger as a certainty, which scanners can establish only statistically, the full extent of a capability, freedom from benchmark contamination, the safety of a signed model, the real parent of an undeclared derivative, and, by proxy (SCRUM-281), anything beyond statements and outputs (Part 5).
