Model-integrity and inference-time threats

What this document answers

An organisation that takes an open-source model (SCRUM-281) into use meets its threats in two states.

  • Before adoption: the model as an object.
    • The organisation that holds the file (SCRUM-281) studies the model's behaviour and its internals.
    • It compares the model with its parent, or with other candidates for the same use.
    • It tries to reveal what was done to the model before the model arrives in service.
    • This is where threats to the model itself are found, if they can be found at all.
    • The state acts as a gate the model must pass.
  • In service: the model through its interface.
    • The organisation watches the inputs that reach the model, the content it reads, and the outputs and actions it returns.
    • This is where threats that act on the model in use appear.
    • They appear as failures of security, harm to the organisation's data and systems, or of safety, the model producing what its safeguard (SCRUM-281) should refuse.
  • The two states are not substitutes.
    • Testing before adoption can search for a trigger, in the model's behaviour, in its internals and against its parent, and can find one; it cannot show that none is there, because a search covers only the triggers it is able to reach.
    • Watching in service sees only the inputs that arrive.
    • An organisation that reaches a model only through a key, by proxy (SCRUM-281), loses most of the first state and relies on the second.

This document sets the threats on that frame and answers four questions, one per row of Table 1.

Part 1. The threats and how each is described

1.1 What the organisation receives, and what can go wrong with it

An organisation that adopts an open-source model (SCRUM-281) receives a file (SCRUM-281) it did not train, from a producer it does not control, and then runs that file on its own inputs. Three things can go wrong on that path.

  • The file is not what it seems. The account, the repository or the file itself carries something other than the model the organisation selected: a program hidden in the serialisation, a parser exploit, a substituted revision. These are the supply-chain and artefact-level threats (SCRUM-281), T1 to T8 of the supply-chain document, Part 2.
  • The file is what it seems, but the model inside it was altered. Someone with access to its training data, its training or its numbers changed what it does, before the organisation received it or while the organisation adjusted it. The file loads cleanly and passes every scan for executable content; what differs is behaviour. These are the model-integrity threats.
  • The model is sound, but it is turned against its use. Someone who can reach its input, directly or through content it reads, makes it produce what neither its producer (SCRUM-281) nor the organisation intended, or reads from its outputs what the organisation did not mean to give away. Nothing in the model changes. These are the inference-time and runtime threats.

This document covers the second and third. The two meet at the safeguard (SCRUM-281): a model-integrity threat can remove or weaken the safeguard for every input, while an inference-time threat defeats it on one input at a time.

Part 1 then fixes how every threat is described, so that the entries in Parts 2 and 3 can be compared. Section 1.2 names the thirteen threats. Sections 1.3 to 1.5 define the three things recorded for each: how far it has been shown, the stage at which it reaches the organisation, and whether it acts at model level or system level. Section 1.6 gives the shape of an entry. Each term is defined here once and used unchanged afterwards.

1.2 The thirteen threats

Where the list comes from. The thirteen threats are the scope the report's structure assigns to its two threat-landscape sections on model-integrity threats and on inference-time and runtime threats (report structure, sections 4.3 and 4.4), named in its words. Three of its paired names are split, because the two halves differ in evidence, in stage or in level: prompt injection into direct and indirect, extraction into model and training data, quantisation into drift and triggered backdoors. Model inversion, which the report structure names beside extraction, is treated with training-data extraction, because what it recovers is training data. The abliterated model (SCRUM-281), a published copy whose refusal behaviour was removed by an edit of its numbers, is treated with fine-tune and LoRA tampering, because it is the same threat reached by a third route.

Where the frameworks name them. Each threat is checked against three frameworks: the OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications [TH‑01]; MITRE ATLAS [TH‑02]; and NIST AI 100-2, the adversarial machine learning taxonomy (NIST AI 100-2) [TH‑03]. The last column of Table 2 gives the entries or sections that name each threat, read from their own text. Ten of the thirteen are named by all three. Quantisation-induced drift and quantisation-triggered backdoors are named by NIST alone, in a closing discussion of quantised models rather than as attack classes of its taxonomy; no OWASP entry and no ATLAS technique mentions quantisation. Merge contamination is named by OWASP alone, as an exploit of collaborative model-merge services; neither ATLAS nor NIST mentions model merging.

1.3 Demonstrated or theoretical

Every threat carries a status that says what the evidence shows. The status is separate from the confidence label, which says how well a claim is sourced: a peer-reviewed laboratory result is established and demonstrated in research, not observed in deployment.

  • Observed in deployment. An official record, a security advisory or a party's statement about its own service, or a research measurement, reports the threat against a deployed system, a production service or a public model repository (SCRUM-281).
  • Demonstrated in research. The threat has been carried out on real models in a research setting, with the researchers playing both attacker and victim, and no record of it in deployment was found.
  • Theoretical. The threat is argued, or shown only on a constructed case that does not reach the model types in scope, or not shown at all.

The literature is the official and research sources the citation policy admits (SCRUM-281). Each result is cited where it is used, by its authors, venue and year, or as a preprint, and resolves to a row of the source log that records what was checked in it. A status rests on what the literature shows, not on a measurement taken for this report or on the date it was read, so it holds for any model and any period the cited work covers, and changes only when the literature does. Press and blogs are not evidence for a status. The techniques executed for this report, the demonstration set (SCRUM-281), are not evidence here either; they are reported beside their own evidence.

A threat may hold different statuses in different parts, for example demonstrated in research for its mechanism and not established for its occurrence in published models; each entry says which part holds which. Each entry gives the demonstrated part and the theoretical part under their own headings, so the line between them is never left to the reader. "Not established" is written wherever no admissible source was found, and is never inferred from a neighbouring case.

1.4 The lifecycle stage a threat enters at

Each threat is tagged with the lifecycle stage (SCRUM-281) at which it enters the organisation's hands. The stages are the four the glossary defines as the points at which a question about a model is answered (SCRUM-281, section 1.4, lifecycle stage), and the report structure assesses at (report structure, section 6.1). The lifecycle assessment framework numbers them and fixes the questions answered at each (SCRUM-284, section 1.3, Table 2):

  • S1 selection: the organisation chooses a model from where it is published and checks it before taking it in;
  • S2 adjustment: the organisation changes the model, by fine-tuning, an adapter, a merge, quantisation or conversion, or has another party change it;
  • S3 deployment: the model is put into service with what surrounds it: serving software, configuration, retrieval, tools, guards and access;
  • S4 runtime detection: the model runs, and what deployment fixed and what arrives at its input are watched.

Every threat carries exactly one entry stage for each route by which it can reach the organisation, and the entry says which route it is. The tag gives four things:

  • enters at: the stage at which the threat enters;
  • first answerable at: the framework question at which it can first be answered, Qn.k (SCRUM-284), and the evidence item that answers it, en.k (SCRUM-284);
  • introduced by: the producer, a third party that published a derivative, the organisation's own adjustment, or whoever reaches the model's input;
  • exposure fixed at, where it differs from the entry stage: the earlier stage whose decisions decide whether the threat can reach the model at all.

A threat introduced before the organisation selects the model, in the producer's training or in a derivative another party published, enters at S1, because selection is where the organisation first holds it. The two families therefore enter at different points:

  • Model-integrity threats enter at S1 or S2. At S1 when they arrive with the model; at S2 when the organisation's own adjustment, or one made on its behalf, introduces them. A threat with both routes, training-data poisoning for one, carries both tags, one per route.
  • Inference-time and runtime threats enter at S4. They arrive with the inputs the model receives in service, and are first answerable at Q4.2, on the inputs arriving and the outputs returned, e4.3 (SCRUM-284). Their exposure is fixed at S3, where deployment decides what surrounds the model and who can reach it (SCRUM-284, Q3.2 and Q3.4). The model's susceptibility to them, the model-level part of 1.5, can be tested earlier, at S1 on the model's behaviour, Q1.6 on e1.9, but the threat itself enters only when the model is in service.

Whether the organisation holds the file or only a key, in possession (SCRUM-281) or by proxy (SCRUM-281), decides which evidence exists to answer the question (SCRUM-284, Table 1); by proxy only declared statements and black-box behaviour exist. Each entry states both cases.

1.5 The boundary between model level and system level

The glossary marks every threat as acting at model level or system level (SCRUM-281). The boundary is drawn here by one test.

Move the same model file to a deployment with nothing around it: no system prompt, no retrieval, no tools, no guard (SCRUM-281), default serving settings. If the threat goes with the file, it is model level. If it stays behind, it is system level.

A model-level threat is a property of the numbers. It is present in every deployment of that file, and a test of the model alone can, in principle, find it. A system-level threat exists because of what surrounds the model: the serving software, the configuration, retrieval, tools and permissions that deployment fixes (SCRUM-284, Q3.2). It is assessed per deployment, not per model.

Many threats have a part on each side. The model's inability to tell an instruction from the data it reads is a property of the model; what an injected instruction can then do depends on the tools it can call. Each entry names the part on each side, and Part 4 sets them side by side.

The boundary is also the line between the two states of adoption. The model-level part of a threat is what examination before adoption can reveal, at S1 and S2, by testing the model and comparing it with its parent or with other candidates (SCRUM-284, e2.4 and e2.5). The system-level part exists only once the model is deployed, and is seen at S3 and S4, through the interface.

The boundary decides three things the later chapters depend on:

  • What an acceptance criterion can require, and when. A model-level property can be required of the model before it is selected; a system-level one can be required only of a deployment, and is re-assessed whenever what surrounds the model changes.
  • Who answers. In possession, the organisation answers for both sides. By proxy, the producer answers for the model and the serving provider (SCRUM-281) for the endpoint, never for the model itself, so a model-level threat cannot be passed to the serving provider.
  • Which control can meet it. A guard placed around the model is a system-level control; it can contain a model-level threat on the inputs it sees, and it does not remove it from the file.

Part 2. Model-integrity threats

Six threats alter the model before the organisation runs it. Each leaves the file (SCRUM-281) in a valid format, loadable without error, so none is visible to the scans that look for executable content in a file; those belong to the supply-chain document, whose artefact layer is the route by which an altered file arrives (supply-chain document, Part 2.2, T3 to T7). What changes is behaviour.

Four of the six act through a derivative model (SCRUM-281), and on the primary model repository (SCRUM-281) derivatives are a large and growing share of what is published: fine-tunes, adapters, quantised copies and merged models, each declaring its parent on its model card (SCRUM-286, section 1.1). The relation is a field the uploader fills in and the platform does not verify, so the parent an assessment starts from is the one the uploader named.

The six are defined in Table 2 (section 1.2) and treated below in that order, T9 to T14.

T9. Training-data poisoning

Enters at: S1, through the training of the producer (SCRUM-281), first answerable at Q1.6 on e1.9 and e1.10; or S2, through data the organisation trains on itself, at Q2.2 on e2.2 and e2.5 (SCRUM-284). Introduced by whoever controls part of the training data.

A model learns what its training data shows it. Poisoning is the placement of examples in that data so that the trained model learns a behaviour the attacker chose. It can enter at each phase of training: the web-scale text of pre-training, the instruction examples that teach the model to follow requests, and the preference data that teaches it which answers are better. The goal may be to degrade the model, to bias it on a topic, or to attach a behaviour to a trigger; the last is T10, and the data is one of its routes.

The organisation cannot inspect the data. What the producer used is a declared (SCRUM-284) statement on the model card, e1.2, and nothing verifies it; among widely used models, only a minority fill the training-data field at all (SCRUM-286, section 2.3).

Demonstrated. Four results, each in a research setting: the first on public training datasets, the other three on models the researchers trained themselves.

  • Web-scale datasets can be poisoned in practice. Two routes, buying expired domains whose content a dataset index still points to, and editing pages shortly before a dataset takes its periodic snapshot, could have poisoned 0.01 per cent of two large image-and-text datasets for about 60 US dollars, and ten public datasets were found open to them (Carlini et al., IEEE S&P 2024) [TH‑04].
  • Instruction tuning is sensitive to a few examples. About a hundred poisoned examples made a chosen phrase produce negative or degenerate output across tasks held out from training, and larger models were more affected, not less (Wan et al., ICML 2023) [TH‑05].
  • Pre-training poisoning persists. Researchers pre-trained models from 600 million to 7 billion parameters from scratch with poisoned data, then applied the post-training that makes a chat model. Poisoning 0.1 per cent of the pre-training data was enough for three of four attack goals, belief manipulation, jailbreaking and prompt stealing among them, to persist; denial of service persisted from 0.001 per cent (Zhang et al., ICLR 2025) [TH‑06].
  • The number of poisoned documents needed may not grow with the model. Two hundred and fifty documents were enough for models from 600 million to 13 billion parameters, whatever the size of the clean data; success followed the count of poisoned documents, not their share (Souly et al. 2025, preprint) [TH‑07].

Theoretical or not established.

  • That any published open-source model (SCRUM-281) was poisoned through its training data is not established: no official record and no research measurement was found.
  • Whether the near-constant count holds above 13 billion parameters is not established; the largest models in the cited study are below that size (Souly et al. 2025, preprint) [TH‑07].
  • The organisation's own S2 data being poisoned is the instruction-tuning case above in mechanism (Wan et al., ICML 2023) [TH‑05]; no record of it in an organisation's adjustment was found.

Organisation exposed: both; by proxy (SCRUM-281) the served model carries whatever its training put in it, and the organisation holds only black-box behaviour, e1.9. Level: model level; the result is in the numbers and goes with the file. Status: demonstrated in research; observed in deployment not established. Label: established (Carlini et al., IEEE S&P 2024; Wan et al., ICML 2023; Zhang et al., ICLR 2025) [TH‑04, TH‑05, TH‑06]; the near-constant count emerging (Souly et al. 2025, preprint) [TH‑07]. Applies to: the generative model row (Wan et al., ICML 2023; Zhang et al., ICLR 2025; Souly et al. 2025, preprint) [TH‑05, TH‑06, TH‑07] and the image-and-text qualifier through its training sets (Carlini et al., IEEE S&P 2024) [TH‑04]; the classification model row, where poisoning is demonstrated in research across spam, malware, sentiment and toxic-comment classifiers, as the NIST taxonomy's survey records (NIST AI 100-2, section 2.3) [TH‑03]; the embedding row not established, since no cited source demonstrates poisoning of an embedding model; every artefact format column, since the change is in the numbers whatever the container. Framework: OWASP LLM04:2025 Data and Model Poisoning [TH‑01]; MITRE ATLAS AML.T0020 Training Data Poisoning [TH‑02].

T10. Backdoors and trojan triggers

Enters at: S1, through the producer or a third party that published the model, first answerable at Q1.6 on e1.9 and e1.10; or S2, through an adjustment made by another party on the organisation's behalf, at Q2.2 on e2.3, e2.4 and e2.5 (SCRUM-284). Introduced by whoever trained or changed the model.

A backdoor is behaviour that appears only when the input contains a trigger: a word, a phrase, a date, a pattern. On every other input the model behaves as expected, which is what makes it hard to find. "Trojan" names the same thing in older literature; this document says backdoor and trigger. Three routes plant one: poisoned training data, T9 with a trigger attached; training carried out by another party, who returns a model with the backdoor in it; and the numbers of a pre-trained model, edited so that the backdoor survives the fine-tuning that follows. Adapters, quantisation and merging are further routes, treated as T11, T13 and T14.

The organisation meets T10 in the form the framework anticipates: at Q2.2 it asks for probes for triggers and planted content, e2.5, and for where the weights and activations moved, e2.4 (SCRUM-284). A probe can find only what it sends, and a trigger is chosen so that ordinary inputs do not contain it.

Demonstrated.

  • Training handed to another party can return a backdoored model. A network trained by an outside party was accurate on the owner's validation data and misbehaved on the trigger: a street-sign detector read a stop sign carrying a small sticker as a speed-limit sign, and the backdoor survived transfer to a new task (Gu et al., IEEE Access 2019) [TH‑09].
  • A backdoor can be planted in pre-trained numbers and survive the victim's fine-tuning. Edited pre-trained language models kept the attacker's behaviour after the downstream training that turns them into classifiers (Kurita et al., ACL 2020) [TH‑10]. This is the case of an organisation that downloads a pre-trained model and adapts it.
  • A backdoor can be planted through preference data. Poisoned human feedback planted a trigger word that made any prompt a jailbreak of the safeguard (SCRUM-281) the model was trained with (Rando and Tramèr, ICLR 2024) [TH‑08].
  • A backdoor can persist through safety training. Models built to write secure code when the prompt says the year is 2023 and exploitable code when it says 2024 kept the behaviour through supervised fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and adversarial training; adversarial training taught the models to recognise their trigger better, and persistence was strongest in the largest models (Hubinger et al. 2024, preprint) (Hubinger et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑11].
  • A search can find a trigger in a classifier. A scanner that inverts the words most likely to form a trigger, given only the model and a few benign samples, detected backdoors in 3,839 language classifiers of seven transformer structures, BERT and GPT among them, across 17 attack types, with over 0.9 detection accuracy in most settings (Liu et al., IEEE S&P 2022) [TH‑13].
  • Detection is fragile. Backdoor detectors evaluated for language models failed when the strength with which the backdoor was trained was varied, so a detector's published success does not carry to backdoors trained differently (Yan et al., EMNLP 2025) [TH‑12].
  • In generative models, recovering a planted trigger is far harder. In a trigger-detection competition on generative language models, the best methods recovered the planted triggers with a recall of about 0.16, comparable to sampling sentences at random from inputs like the training prefixes (Maloyan et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑14].

Theoretical or not established.

  • A trigger backdoor in a published open-source model is not established. The malicious models found on the Hugging Face Hub carried code that runs when the file is loaded, not behaviour planted in the numbers (Zhao et al., ASE 2024) [SC‑17]; that is malicious serialisation, T3 of the supply-chain document (Part 2.2), and not this threat.
  • A backdoor that arises without anyone planting it is theoretical. The persistent backdoors above were built deliberately, as studies of what such behaviour would do (Hubinger et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑11].
  • A method that finds an unknown trigger in a generative model reliably is not established: the search that works on classifiers (Liu et al., IEEE S&P 2022) [TH‑13] has no counterpart of that strength for generative models in the cited sources (Yan et al., EMNLP 2025; Maloyan et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑12, TH‑14].

Organisation exposed: both; by proxy the organisation holds only black-box behaviour, e1.9, and a trigger it does not know is absent from its inputs by construction. Level: model level; the backdoor is in the numbers and survives the organisation's own fine-tuning (Kurita et al., ACL 2020; Hubinger et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑10, TH‑11]. Status: demonstrated in research; observed in deployment not established. Label: established (Rando and Tramèr, ICLR 2024; Gu et al., IEEE Access 2019; Kurita et al., ACL 2020; Yan et al., EMNLP 2025; Liu et al., IEEE S&P 2022) [TH‑08, TH‑09, TH‑10, TH‑12, TH‑13]; persistence through safety training and the difficulty of trigger recovery in generative models emerging (Hubinger et al. 2024, preprint; Maloyan et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑11, TH‑14]. Applies to: the classification model row (Gu et al., IEEE Access 2019; Kurita et al., ACL 2020) [TH‑09, TH‑10] and the generative model row (Wan et al., ICML 2023; Rando and Tramèr, ICLR 2024; Hubinger et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑05, TH‑08, TH‑11]; the embedding row not established, since no cited source demonstrates a backdoor in an embedding model; every artefact format column. The NIST taxonomy records backdoors in transformer classifiers for sentiment and toxic-comment detection (NIST AI 100-2, section 2.3) [TH‑03]. Framework: OWASP LLM04:2025 Data and Model Poisoning [TH‑01]; MITRE ATLAS AML.T0018.000 Poison AI Model, under AML.T0018 Manipulate AI Model, and AML.T0020 Training Data Poisoning for the data route [TH‑02].

T11. Fine-tune and LoRA tampering

Enters at: S1, through a third party that published a fine-tuned, adapted or abliterated copy, first answerable at Q1.6 on e1.9 and e1.10; or S2, through the organisation's own fine-tune or adapter, at Q2.3 on e2.5 against e1.9 and on e2.4 (SCRUM-284). Introduced by whoever changed the model after its producer (SCRUM-281).

A published model can be changed by anyone who holds its file. Three routes change it: further training on new examples, a fine-tune; a small adapter (SCRUM-281) loaded beside the model, of which LoRA is the common kind; and a direct edit of the numbers. The change can remove the safeguard (SCRUM-281), plant a behaviour, or both. The abliterated model (SCRUM-281) is the direct-edit route made public: a copy whose refusal was located and removed, then published. The adapter is also a file kind with its own delivery threat, the malicious adapter of the supply-chain document (Part 2.2, T6); this entry concerns what the adapter does to behaviour, not the code it may carry.

Demonstrated.

  • Fine-tuning removes the safeguard cheaply, and can do so without intent. Ten harmful examples, submitted through a production fine-tuning service, removed the safety alignment of the served model, and fine-tuning on benign data also degraded it (Qi et al., ICLR 2024) [TH‑15].
  • An adapter removes it on the largest open models. A LoRA adapter brought the refusal rate of a 70-billion parameter chat model to about one per cent on two refusal benchmarks, on one GPU, keeping its other capabilities (Lermen et al. 2023, preprint) [TH‑16].
  • A direct edit removes it without training. Refusal in thirteen open chat models up to 72 billion parameters is carried by a single direction inside the model, and a rank-one edit of the numbers that removes the direction removes refusal (Arditi et al., NeurIPS 2024) [TH‑17].
  • Abliterated copies are published. The primary model repository (SCRUM-281) lists repositories that name themselves abliterated, under the platform's own search (Hugging Face Hub search) [TH‑20].
  • Adapters can carry a backdoor that survives sharing. A backdoored LoRA adapter kept its behaviour when combined with other adapters (Liu et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑18]; trojaned adapters made a model emit the attacker's content or misuse its tools, running malware and writing spear-phishing, and none of three defences tested removed the threat fully (Dong et al., NDSS 2025) [TH‑19].

Theoretical or not established.

  • How often published fine-tunes and adapters carry a planted behaviour, as opposed to a removed safeguard, is not established: no measurement was found.
  • Whether a parent's assessment says anything about a derivative is not established in either direction; the glossary therefore treats every derivative as a model of its own (SCRUM-281).

Organisation exposed: both; by proxy the serving provider (SCRUM-281) may serve a fine-tune or adapter the organisation cannot inspect, and the organisation holds only behaviour, e1.9. Level: model level; the change is in the numbers or in the adapter loaded with them. Status: observed in deployment, for safeguard removal through a production fine-tuning service (Qi et al., ICLR 2024) [TH‑15] and for published abliterated copies (Hugging Face Hub search) [TH‑20]; demonstrated in research for planted adapter behaviour (Liu et al. 2024, preprint; Dong et al., NDSS 2025) [TH‑18, TH‑19]. Label: established (Qi et al., ICLR 2024; Arditi et al., NeurIPS 2024; Dong et al., NDSS 2025; Hugging Face Hub search) [TH‑15, TH‑17, TH‑19, TH‑20]; the adapter results on the largest models and on combined adapters emerging (Lermen et al. 2023, preprint; Liu et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑16, TH‑18]. Applies to: the generative model row, instruct and chat models chiefly, since they carry the safeguard (SCRUM-281); the adapter file kind; every artefact format column; the classification and embedding rows not established in the cited sources. Framework: as Table 2.

T12. Quantisation-induced drift

Enters at: S1, through a third party that published a quantised copy, first answerable at Q1.6 on e1.9; or S2, through the organisation's own quantisation, at Q2.3 and Q2.4 on e2.5 against e1.9 (SCRUM-284). Introduced by whoever quantised the model, without intent.

A quantised model (SCRUM-281) stores the same model with less precise numbers, so that it runs on less memory. Its behaviour is close to its parent's and not identical, and nothing guarantees that the safeguard moves with the rest. Drift is the unintended difference: no one planted it, and no one checked it.

Demonstrated.

  • The official taxonomy records the effect: quantised models inherit the weaknesses of the original and add their own, since the loss of precision amplifies errors and weakens robustness to adversarial inputs, and the effect on generative models has been studied less than on predictive ones (NIST AI 100-2, section 4.2.4) (NIST AI 100-2) [TH‑03].
  • On open generative models, fine-tuning raised jailbreak success while quantisation had mixed effects, in either direction depending on the model and the method (Kumar et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑21].

Theoretical or not established.

  • The direction and size of the drift for a given model and quantisation method cannot be predicted from the parent: not established. It is measured per copy or not known.
  • That drift has caused harm in a deployment is not established.

Organisation exposed: both; by proxy the serving provider chooses the precision it serves, and the organisation may not know it. Level: model level. Status: demonstrated in research. Label: established for the effect stated by the taxonomy (NIST AI 100-2) [TH‑03]; its direction on generative models emerging (Kumar et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑21]. Applies to: the quantised model file kind, in every model type row; the GGUF column chiefly, where quantisation is the normal state of the file (SCRUM-280, Table 6). Framework: as Table 2.

T13. Quantisation-triggered backdoors

Enters at: S1, through the producer or a third party that published the full-precision model, first answerable once a quantised copy exists: at Q2.3 and Q2.4 on e2.5 against e1.9 when the organisation quantises at S2, or at Q1.6 when it selects a quantised copy (SCRUM-284). Introduced by whoever built the model.

A model can be built to behave well at full precision and badly once it is quantised. The behaviour is planted in the rounding: the full-precision numbers pass every test, and the quantised copy, which is what is deployed on smaller hardware, does what the attacker chose. This is T10 with quantisation as its trigger, and T12 made deliberate.

Demonstrated.

  • On generative models, a full-precision model that appeared benign became malicious once quantised with widely used methods, producing insecure code, injecting content or over-refusing (Egashira et al., NeurIPS 2024) [TH‑22]; the official taxonomy records the result (NIST AI 100-2, section 4.2.4) [TH‑03].
  • The same holds for the quantisation types of the GGUF format: across three generative models and nine GGUF types, the quantised copies produced insecure code in 88.7 per cent and injected content in 85.0 per cent of cases (Egashira et al., ICML 2025) [TH‑23].
  • On classifiers, standard post-training quantisation in commercial deployment frameworks activated a dormant backdoor with close to complete success (Ma et al. 2021, preprint) [TH‑24], and training aware of quantisation made quantisation cause accuracy loss, targeted errors or a backdoor (Hong et al., NeurIPS 2021) [TH‑25].

Theoretical or not established.

  • A published model carrying a quantisation-triggered backdoor is not established.
  • A test of the full-precision model alone cannot find it; it is found, if at all, by testing the quantised copy against its parent, the comparison of 1.5.

Organisation exposed: both; by proxy only behaviour exists, and the precision served is the serving provider's choice. Level: model level. Status: demonstrated in research. Label: established (NIST AI 100-2; Egashira et al., NeurIPS 2024; Egashira et al., ICML 2025; Hong et al., NeurIPS 2021) [TH‑03, TH‑22, TH‑23, TH‑25]; the commercial-framework result emerging (Ma et al. 2021, preprint) [TH‑24]. Applies to: the quantised model file kind; the generative model row (Egashira et al., NeurIPS 2024; Egashira et al., ICML 2025) [TH‑22, TH‑23] and the classification row (Ma et al. 2021, preprint; Hong et al., NeurIPS 2021) [TH‑24, TH‑25]; the GGUF column demonstrated (Egashira et al., ICML 2025) [TH‑23], other columns through the quantisation method rather than the container. Framework: as Table 2.

T14. Merge contamination

Enters at: S1, through a third party that published a merged model, first answerable at Q1.6 on e1.9; or S2, when the organisation merges models itself, at Q2.2 and Q2.3 on e2.5 against each parent's e1.9 (SCRUM-284). Introduced by whoever published one of the parents.

A merged model (SCRUM-281) combines the numbers of two or more models. Each parent contributes what it carries, so a parent with a backdoor or an unwanted behaviour can pass it into the merge, and the merged model has no parent's assessment.

Demonstrated.

  • One backdoored contributing model compromised the whole merged model, whatever the merging coefficients (Zhang et al., ACM CCS 2024) [TH‑26].
  • On generative models, a malicious model uploaded for others to merge passed its backdoor to the merged model, which kept its usefulness, and the backdoor survived paraphrasing defences and fine-pruning (Yuan et al., ACL 2025) [TH‑27].
  • A model built to be merged raised the leakage of personal data and of training-set membership in the merged result (Guo et al. 2025, preprint) [TH‑28].
  • The industry framework records merging as a route around review, merged models being common and highly ranked on public leaderboards (OWASP LLM03:2025) [TH‑01].

Theoretical or not established.

  • That a merged model inherits a parent's contamination of evaluation data, so that its benchmark scores overstate it, is theoretical: no research or official source establishes it.
  • A published merged model carrying a planted backdoor is not established.

Organisation exposed: both. Level: model level. Status: demonstrated in research for inherited backdoors and leakage; theoretical for inherited evaluation contamination. Label: established (Zhang et al., ACM CCS 2024; Yuan et al., ACL 2025) [TH‑01, TH‑26, TH‑27]; leakage emerging (Guo et al. 2025, preprint) [TH‑28]. Applies to: the merged model file kind; the generative model row (Yuan et al., ACL 2025; Guo et al. 2025, preprint) [TH‑27, TH‑28]; other rows not established in the cited sources; every artefact format column. Framework: as Table 2.

Part 3. Inference-time and runtime threats

Seven threats act on a model in service through what reaches its input or what leaves its output. Each is defined in Table 2 (section 1.2) and treated below in that order, T15 to T21. All seven enter at S4, with their exposure fixed at S3 (1.4). Each has a part that goes with the file (SCRUM-281), the model's susceptibility, and a part that stays with the deployment, what the deployment lets the attacker reach; each entry names both.

T15. Direct prompt injection

Enters at: S4, through the person writing the input, first answerable at Q4.2 on e4.3; exposure fixed at S3, Q3.2, by the instructions and data the deployment places in front of the model (SCRUM-284).

A deployment gives the model instructions the user does not see: a system prompt, a role, rules. Direct injection is an input that overrides them, so that the model follows the user instead of the deployment, or reveals what the deployment put in front of it.

Demonstrated.

  • Inputs that hijack the task and leak the hidden instructions worked against a production model's interface (Perez and Ribeiro, NeurIPS ML Safety Workshop 2022) [TH‑29].
  • Five attack kinds and ten defences evaluated on ten models showed the attacks succeed across models and no defence stopped them fully (Liu et al., USENIX Security 2024) [TH‑30].
  • Records of deployment: a service's prompt injection let a user take over its logic and leak its hard-coded system prompt (CVE-2024-5184) [TH‑31]; in an application framework, a prompt injection executed arbitrary code through the component that ran the model's output (CVE-2023-29374) [TH‑32].
  • Training the model to rank the deployment's instructions above the user's made it more robust, with some over-refusal (Wallace et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑33]: the susceptibility is a property of the model.

Theoretical or not established.

  • A model that cannot be directly injected is not established; the cited defences reduce the rate only.

Organisation exposed: both. Level: model level for the susceptibility (Wallace et al. 2024, preprint) [TH‑33]; system level for the consequence, which is whatever the deployment's instructions protect and its output can reach (CVE-2024-5184; CVE-2023-29374) [TH‑31, TH‑32]. Status: observed in deployment. Label: established. Applies to: the generative model row, instruct and chat models; every artefact format column. Framework: as Table 2.

T16. Indirect prompt injection

Enters at: S4, through content the model reads, a page, a document, an e-mail, a tool's output, first answerable at Q4.2 on e4.3; exposure fixed at S3, Q3.2, by the retrieval and tools that bring content to the model (SCRUM-284).

When the model reads content the attacker wrote, instructions inside that content are followed as if they came from the user. The attacker never talks to the model; they place text where the model will read it.

Demonstrated.

  • Instructions planted in content read by production assistants, a search-integrated chat and code completion, led to data theft and to injections that spread themselves (Greshake et al., ACM AISec 2023) (Greshake et al., ACM AISec 2023) [TH‑34].
  • Records of deployment: an injection in an office assistant disclosed information over a network (CVE-2025-32711) [TH‑35]; in a coding agent, an indirect injection wrote the agent's tool configuration and ran code without the user's approval (CVE-2025-54135) [TH‑36]; in another coding assistant, a web-fetching tool reached files outside the workspace, so that an indirect injection could exfiltrate them (CVE-2025-66389) [TH‑37].
  • The official taxonomy states that current mitigations do not fully protect against indirect injection, and advises designing the system on the assumption that it will happen (NIST AI 100-2, section 3.4) [TH‑03].

Theoretical or not established.

  • A model that reliably separates instructions from the data it reads is not established.

Organisation exposed: both. Level: model level for the susceptibility, which no cited model is free of; system level for the exposure and the consequence, which retrieval, tools and permissions decide. Status: observed in deployment. Label: established; that the deployment records, on hosted assistants, describe a susceptibility shared by open-source models (SCRUM-281) is an extension, emerging. Applies to: the generative model row, with the image-and-text qualifier where content arrives as images; every artefact format column. Framework: as Table 2.

T17. Jailbreaks

Enters at: S4, through the person writing the input, first answerable at Q4.2 on e4.3; exposure fixed at S3, Q3.2, by the serving settings and guards (SCRUM-284). For an open-source model the attacker can also hold the file, and prepare the jailbreak offline.

A jailbreak is an input that makes the model produce what its safeguard (SCRUM-281) was trained to refuse. It differs from T11 in that nothing in the model changes: the same file, given another input, refuses again.

Demonstrated.

  • Safety training fails in two systematic ways, a request that sets the model's goals against each other and one phrased where the training did not reach, and attacks built on them succeeded on production models (Wei et al., NeurIPS 2023) [TH‑38].
  • Suffixes optimised on open models transferred to production services (Zou et al. 2023, preprint) [TH‑39]; hundreds of examples placed in a long input jailbroke widely used models, with success growing as a power law of their number (Anil et al., NeurIPS 2024) [TH‑40]; a template with random search reached complete success on open and production models (Andriushchenko et al., ICLR 2025) [TH‑41].
  • On open models, changing only the generation settings raised the success rate from near zero to over 95 per cent on eleven models (Huang et al., ICLR 2024) [TH‑42]; with the file, the refusal direction itself can be removed at inference (Arditi et al., NeurIPS 2024) [TH‑17].

Theoretical or not established.

  • A safeguard that resists all jailbreaks is not established; every cited defence is broken by a later attack in the cited work.

Organisation exposed: both; in possession (SCRUM-281) the organisation also sets the generation settings that one route exploits (Huang et al., ICLR 2024) [TH‑42]. Level: model level, since the safeguard is in the numbers; the generation-settings route is system level, set by the serving configuration. Status: observed in deployment, through research on production services (Wei et al., NeurIPS 2023; Andriushchenko et al., ICLR 2025) [TH‑38, TH‑41]. Label: established; transfer emerging (Zou et al. 2023, preprint) [TH‑39]. Applies to: the generative model row, instruct and chat models; every artefact format column. Framework: as Table 2.

T18. Model extraction

Enters at: S4, through whoever can query the endpoint, first answerable at Q4.4 on e4.5, the access record; exposure fixed at S3, Q3.4, by who can reach the model and what its outputs expose (SCRUM-284).

Extraction reconstructs a model's parameters or behaviour from its outputs. An open-source model is already public, so extraction matters for what the organisation adds: a model it fine-tuned, adapted or merged at S2, served to others, is its own, and its outputs can rebuild it.

Demonstrated.

  • Logistic regressions, neural networks and decision trees were extracted almost exactly through the prediction interfaces of production machine-learning services (Tramèr et al., USENIX Security 2016) (Tramèr et al., USENIX Security 2016) [TH‑43].
  • Part of production language models, the final projection layer and the hidden size, was recovered through their public interface at low cost (Carlini et al., ICML 2024) [TH‑44].

Theoretical or not established.

  • Full extraction of a large generative model's parameters through its interface is not established; the cited work recovers one layer.

Organisation exposed: by proxy (SCRUM-281) the serving provider (SCRUM-281) answers for the endpoint, never for the model, and the organisation that serves its own adjusted model is the party exposed. Level: system level; what can be extracted is decided by what the endpoint returns and to whom. Status: observed in deployment, through research on production services. Label: established. Applies to: every model type row, classification first (Tramèr et al., USENIX Security 2016) [TH‑43], generative (Carlini et al., ICML 2024) [TH‑44]; artefact formats do not apply, since the attack needs only outputs. Framework: as Table 2.

T19. Training-data extraction

Enters at: S4, through whoever can query the model, first answerable at Q4.2 on e4.3; exposure fixed at S2, when the organisation trains or fine-tunes on its own data, and at S3, Q3.4 (SCRUM-284).

A model can return data it was trained on, verbatim, or that data can be reconstructed from its outputs; inversion is the reconstruction case. For an open-source model the producer's data is at stake; for the organisation's own fine-tune, its own data is.

Demonstrated.

  • Hundreds of verbatim training sequences, personal data among them, were extracted from a public generative model (Carlini et al., USENIX Security 2021) [TH‑45].
  • A production chat model was made to emit training data at about 150 times its normal rate, and thousands of examples were recovered (Nasr et al., ICLR 2025) [TH‑46].
  • Inversion reconstructed recognisable faces from a name and access to a face classifier's confidence values (Fredrikson et al., ACM CCS 2015) [TH‑47]; from text embeddings, 92 per cent of 32-token inputs were recovered exactly, full names from clinical notes among them (Morris et al., EMNLP 2023) [TH‑48].

Theoretical or not established.

  • Which records of a given model can be extracted cannot be predicted from the data alone: not established.

Organisation exposed: both. Level: model level for memorisation, which is in the numbers; system level for exposure, which the outputs returned and the access granted decide, embeddings stored and served included. Status: observed in deployment, through research on a production service (Nasr et al., ICLR 2025) [TH‑46]. Label: established. Applies to: the generative model row (Carlini et al., USENIX Security 2021; Nasr et al., ICLR 2025) [TH‑45, TH‑46], the classification row (Fredrikson et al., ACM CCS 2015) [TH‑47] and the embedding row (Morris et al., EMNLP 2023) [TH‑48]; every artefact format column. Framework: as Table 2.

T20. Membership inference

Enters at: S4, through whoever can query the model, first answerable at Q4.2 on e4.3; exposure fixed at S2, by the data trained on, and at S3, Q3.4 and the outputs the endpoint returns (SCRUM-284).

Membership inference decides from a model's outputs whether a given record was in its training data. It reveals the fact of inclusion, a patient in a clinical dataset for example, without extracting the record.

Demonstrated.

  • Shadow models trained to imitate the target inferred membership against classifiers built on production machine-learning services, a hospital dataset among them (Shokri et al., IEEE S&P 2017) [TH‑49].
  • On generative models trained on large public corpora, membership inference barely beat random guessing, and apparent successes came from differences between the member and non-member data rather than from membership (Duan et al., COLM 2024) [TH‑50].

Theoretical or not established.

  • Reliable membership inference against a large pre-trained generative model is not established (Duan et al., COLM 2024) [TH‑50]. Whether it holds for a model fine-tuned on a small dataset is not established in the cited sources.

Organisation exposed: both. Level: model level for what the numbers retain; system level for what the outputs expose, such as confidence values. Status: observed in deployment on classifiers, through research on production services (Shokri et al., IEEE S&P 2017) [TH‑49]; not established on large generative models (Duan et al., COLM 2024) [TH‑50]. Label: established. Applies to: the classification row (Shokri et al., IEEE S&P 2017) [TH‑49]; the generative row with the limit above (Duan et al., COLM 2024) [TH‑50]; every artefact format column. Framework: as Table 2.

T21. Agentic and tool-use abuse

Enters at: S4, through an input or content that reaches an agent, first answerable at Q4.2 on e4.3, the inputs and the actions returned; exposure fixed at S3, Q3.2 and Q3.4, by the tools, permissions and autonomy the deployment grants (SCRUM-284).

An agent is a deployment in which the model calls tools, browses, runs code or acts on the organisation's behalf. Agentic abuse makes it take an action no one authorised. It is usually reached through T15 or T16, and its consequence is whatever the tools allow.

Demonstrated.

  • In a benchmark of 97 tasks and 629 security cases, injected content made agents take attacker-chosen actions in a share of cases even against the best agents tested (Debenedetti et al., NeurIPS 2024) [TH‑51]; across 30 agents, a tool-integrated agent built on a leading model was attacked successfully in about a quarter of cases, and in about half with a reinforcing prompt (Zhan et al., Findings of ACL 2024) [TH‑52].
  • Trojaned adapters made an agent run malware and send spear-phishing (Dong et al., NDSS 2025) [TH‑19]: the susceptibility can be planted in the model.
  • Records of deployment: an indirect injection made a coding agent change its own tool configuration and run code without approval (CVE-2025-54135) [TH‑36]; a web-fetching tool exposed files to exfiltration (CVE-2025-66389) [TH‑37].

Theoretical or not established.

  • An agent design that prevents unauthorised actions without limiting its tools is not established; the official taxonomy treats agents as exposed to every attack on the model plus those on their tools (NIST AI 100-2, section 3.5) [TH‑03].

Organisation exposed: both; by proxy the serving provider answers for its endpoint, and the organisation for the tools it connects. Level: system level, decided by the tools, permissions and autonomy granted; model level where the behaviour is planted (Dong et al., NDSS 2025) [TH‑19]. Status: observed in deployment. Label: established. Applies to: the generative model row, instruct and chat models; every artefact format column. Framework: as Table 2.

Summary: the four questions answered

Which threats have been shown to work, and which are still argued? Six of the thirteen are observed in deployment: safeguard (SCRUM-281) removal by fine-tuning, through a production service and in published abliterated copies (T11); direct and indirect prompt injection, recorded in security advisories (T15, T16); jailbreaks, model extraction and training-data extraction, shown against production services (T17 to T19); and agentic abuse, recorded in advisories (T21). Membership inference is observed on classifiers and not established as reliable on large generative models (T20). Five are demonstrated in research and not established in any published model: poisoning, backdoors, quantisation drift, quantisation-triggered backdoors and merged backdoors (T9, T10, T12 to T14). What is theoretical is narrower: a backdoor that arises without being planted (T10) and evaluation contamination inherited through a merge (T14). Ten threats are named by all three frameworks, the two quantisation threats by NIST alone, and merge contamination by OWASP alone (Table 2).

At which point does each threat enter? The six model-integrity threats enter at selection, with the model, or at adjustment, through the organisation's own change or one made on its behalf; the quantisation-triggered backdoor enters at selection and can be answered only once a quantised copy exists. The seven inference-time and runtime threats enter in service, with their exposure fixed at deployment, and for training-data extraction and membership inference also at adjustment, by the data the organisation trains on (Table 3).

Can the threat be judged on the model, or only on the deployed system? By one test: what goes with the file is model level, what stays with the deployment is system level (1.5). T9 to T14 are model level and can be required of the model before it is selected or after it is adjusted. Model extraction is system level and can be required only of a deployment. The other six are split: the model's susceptibility can be tested before adoption, and what it leads to is decided by the deployment, so both a criterion on the model and a criterion on the deployment are needed (Table 4). Examination before adoption can search for a trigger and, in classifiers, find one, but cannot show that none is there, and recovering a planted trigger in a generative model is not reliable with any method the literature establishes; observation in service is therefore not optional.

Which threats must the threat classification and decision cycle decide on? All thirteen, each with one row in Table 3 carrying its family, entry stage, level, exposure, model types, status, confidence label, framework entries and sources, and an empty Disposition column for the decision.
